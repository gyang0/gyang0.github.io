A learner of Ancient Greek, specifically the Attic dialect, will know that there are three main flavors of 1st declension nouns. Nouns ending in long alpha or eta are relatively common (ex. <em>ἡσυχίᾱ, -ᾶς</em> or <em>σκηνή, -ῆς</em>). However, there also exists a short-alpha subset that's identical in form to the long-alpha nouns, except with a short alpha in the singular nominative, accusative, and vocative (ex. <em>θάλατταν, -ης</em>).

Though it seems random, there's actually a beautifully logical reason behind these distinctions that goes all the way back to Proto Indo-European, circa 4500 &ndash; 2500 BCE. Thanks to Professor Olga Levaniouk at the University of Washington, who explained all this to me in office hours. This post is based on her explanations, and all mistakes are mine, not hers.

## Proto Indo-European
### History
Proto Indo-European is the "mother language" of most Western languages today. Though we have no written samples, pronunciation can be reconstructed by linguistics who study the evolution of its child languages.

### Laryngeals
Proto Indo-European had *laryngeals*, sounds hypothesized to exist in their language. Denoted by *h&#8321;*, *h&#8322;*, and *h&#8323;*. They had distinct pronunciations that I can't pronounce myself, but Wikipedia has some reconstructions.

- *h&#8321;* could have been similar to the <a target="_blank" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_glottal_fricative">voiceless glottal fricative</a> *[h]*, or a <a target="_blank" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glottal_stop">glottal stop</a> *[ʔ]*. Like the constricting of the throat in "ah-ah."
- *h&#8322;* could have been similar to a <a target="_blank" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_velar_fricative">voiceless velar fricative</a> *[x]*. Like "sash."
- *h&#8323;* could have been similar to a <a target="_blank" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close-mid_central_rounded_vowel">close-mid central rounded vowel</a> *[ɵ]*, similar to "ooh" with more emphasis.

In PIE's child languages, the laryngeals were usually lost or absorbed into the sounds of neighboring letters. It could also affect the length of neighboring vowels, so you can probably see how this ties into Greek.

## In Ancient Greek
### A Hint of Laryngeals
PIE had a distinction in its words: thematic vs athematic. There was kind of a reason for the distinction &ndash; whether something was more "active" or "acted on" &ndash; but the distinction became more and more muddled.

However, we can still see traces of it in Greek. In fact, the long-alpha vs short-alpha in Greek's 1st declension feminine nouns comes from PIE's distinction of thematic vs. athematic nouns. The PIE ending of *eh&#8322;* became ᾱ, while *h&#8322;* became α.

The laryngeals also had other effects on Greek. For example, some Greek verbs have an e/o/zero-grading system in its vowels:

The lengthening or changing of vowels like above can hint to laryngeals in PIE words that were dropped in Greek, but retained linguistic features.

### In Other Languages
In other languages, the laryngeals tended to be dropped entirely. For example, Proto Indo-European had **h₂stḗr* for star. In Greek, the laryngeal turned into a vowel, becoming *ἄστρα*. In other languages, the laryngeal was dropped entirely, hence we get the English "star," not "astra" or similar. As another example, compare the Greek *ὄνομα* (name) with the Latin *nomen*. Greek keeps a hint of the laryngeal as a vowel, while Latin has dropped it.

Hittite was a language that kept the laryngeals. Most languages &ndash; Greek, Latin, Sanskrit, for example &ndash; lost them, leaving behind only traces like the Greek vowel lengths. In Armenian (another stubborn, mostly unchanging language like Greek), some instances of *h&#8322;* survive as *h*.
